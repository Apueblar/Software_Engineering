Maciej Piasecki, G4.19, Department of AI, WUST  Artificial Intelligence and Knowledge Engineering   2  Games:   basic   concepts  •   Game – a sequence of actions led by the player (or players)  in accordance with prescribed rules to reach a specific goal  •   Game strategy – a complete set of rules that determines  the player's move, chosen for game situations  •   Formal definition of the game (von Neumann and  Morgenstern) Game Theory):  „The game consists of a set of rules governing the  possibilities of choice in a situation referred to as conflict  of interest, in which each unit is trying to maximize its own  profit and minimizing the profit of other entities (players).  Game rules also define the amount of information available  to each player and the number of winners and losers.”

Maciej Piasecki, G4.19, Department of AI, WUST  Artificial Intelligence and Knowledge Engineering   3  Tasks   of   players  •   Games require players  •   to make a series of decisions that increase their  expected   payoff   (the amount of winnings)  •   and to take into account the factors determining the  current situation ( game state ) and, usually, a limited  time for actions  •   Temporal restrictions do not   normally allow for taking  optimal decisions  •   Undertaken decisions cannot be withdrawn  •   The long-term outcome of the undertaken decisions is  uncertain, because the decisions of the other players are  unknown

Maciej Piasecki, G4.19, Department of AI, WUST  Artificial Intelligence and Knowledge Engineering   4  Game   types  •   Number of players:  •   zero-player games, e.g., the game of life (John Conway's Game of  Life)  •   one-player games, e.g., puzzle, solitaire  •   two-player games, e.g., chess, draughts (checkers), Othello, Go  •   multiplayer games, e.g., Halma, poker, and bridge  •   Winning and losing conditions:  •   zero-sum games, where the win of one player is the loss of the  second one (zero-sum games), e.g., chess, checkers, Othello, and Go  •   non-zero sum, e.g., prisoner dilemma  •   Cooperation of players:  •   cooperative – players cooperate, e.g. bridge  •   non-cooperative – players do not cooperate  •   Elements of randomness:  •   completely random game, e.g. roulette  •   partially random games, e.g., bridge and other card games  •   deterministic games, e.g., checkers, chess, Othello, and Go

Maciej Piasecki, G4.19, Department of AI, WUST  Artificial Intelligence and Knowledge Engineering   8  Game   tree  •   In every step of the game, players perform their moves  selected from the set of possible moves  •   The game can be seen as a search for the player’s  movements that ensure his victory  •   A tree can describe this process of searching  •   the   root   is the   initial state   of the game,  •   and the   nodes   —   states   resulting from the subsequent  moves, possible at the given game state  •   Even for simple games, the trees are very complicated, so  different methods are used to assess the current state of  the game and choose the next moves, without having to  build the whole tree  •   The quality of individual nodes, i.e., states of the game, is  defined by a function that evaluates each node –  a   heuristic evaluation function

Maciej Piasecki, G4.19, Department of AI, WUST  Artificial Intelligence and Knowledge Engineering   12  Brute-force   (non-)solutions  •   Blind search — searching through the game tree without a  heuristic function — “brute force”  •   All nodes of a game tree accessible from a given position  are expanded and checked  •   Concept of game tree — the analogy of solving problems  by humans  •   A human, while considering hypothetical scenarios  •   does not take into account all possible events  •   eliminates from consideration unlikely events, or those  assessed at the beginning as not leading to the goal  •   Very large scale of the problem,  •   e.g. for chess 35 100   search tree nodes with 10 40  different possible situations on the board

Maciej Piasecki, G4.19, Department of AI, WUST  Artificial Intelligence and Knowledge Engineering   13  Min-Max   Algorithm  •   Evaluation function   ( heuristic function ) — additional knowledge derived  from the problem  •   compatible with the final state  •   should be positively correlated with ‘the chances for winning’  •   Unknown exact value of the evaluation function  •   discovering it is a challenge  •   Min-max idea: choosing movements leading to the higher-scoring states  •   During the game:  •   the opponent tries to choose actions leading to his victory  •   important assumption:   his topmost game-playing skills  •   ➠   he selects the worst moves for us (Min!)  •   Always looking at the problem from the perspective of `our’ player:  •   one should choose those operators that lead to the states evaluated  as the best ones ( Max )  •   his opponent will select operators leading to conditions that  minimise the value of the evaluation function ( Min )  •   ➠   Min-Max Algorithm

Maciej Piasecki, G4.19, Department of AI, WUST  Artificial Intelligence and Knowledge Engineering   26  Deep   Blue   —   the   chess   chip   (processor)  Move generation  an 8   ×   8 array of combinatorial logic — a hardwired finite state machine  generates only one move at a time — from all possible ones  "move ordering, preferably as close to best-first as possible”  “first generating captures (ordered from low-valued pieces capturing high-valued  pieces to high-valued capturing low-valued), followed by non- capture moves (ordered  by centrality).”  Evaluation function  fast evaluation  mostly “basic piece values with square-based location adjustments”  and slow evaluation  “chess concepts such as square control, pins, X-rays, king safety, pawn structure,  passed pawns, ray control, outposts, pawn majority, rook on the 7th, blockade,  restraint, color complex, trapped pieces, development, and so on.”  Search control  4-5 ply with quiescence (midgame), deeper in end game  “a fixed-depth null-window search, which includes a quiescence search”  a repetition detector  “a 32-entry circular buffer of the last 32 ply.”  “the numbers of pieces displaced in each of the last 32 positions”
